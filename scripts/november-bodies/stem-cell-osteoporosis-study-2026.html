This fall, a headline went around claiming that a "living medication" restored bone strength in women with osteoporosis. Another said fractures fell by 94 percent. I understand why those stories got shared. When you live with this disease, a treatment that rebuilds bone from your own cells sounds like exactly what you've been hoping for.

I read the study itself. It's interesting science, and I hope it goes somewhere. But it's very early, and it isn't a reason to put off the treatment that already works.

What the Researchers Did

The study was published in September in Cell, one of the most respected science journals. A team in Murcia, Spain, working with researchers in Miami, treated 10 women aged 51 to 72. All had advanced osteoporosis and had already broken at least one bone.

Each woman had a small amount of bone marrow drawn from her hip. In the lab, the researchers grew a type of stem cell from that marrow and gave the cells a temporary sugar coating designed to help them find their way to bone. Then each woman got her own cells back in a single IV infusion.

This was a first-in-human safety study. Its main job is to find out whether a treatment is safe enough to keep studying.

What They Found

Over the two years after the infusion, the group as a whole had far fewer fractures than in the two years before. That's where the 94 percent figure comes from. In plain numbers, about 16 fractures before became 1 fracture after.

Bone biopsies from the hip, taken four months later, showed about 70 percent more bone tissue on average. Blood tests suggested more bone was being built, and pain scores went down.

What didn't change is worth knowing too. On a standard DEXA scan, the same test you and I get, bone density at the spine and hip didn't change in a meaningful way.

On safety, side effects were mostly minor, like soreness where the marrow was drawn. Two women were hospitalized for problems the researchers judged unrelated to the cells, and no tumors turned up over about six years of follow-up. That's encouraging. But 10 people is far too few to rule out rare risks.

Why the 94 Percent Is Shaky

There was no comparison group. Without one, there's no way to know how much of the drop in fractures came from the cells and how much came from other things.

And there were other things. Only two of the ten women had never taken an osteoporosis medicine. The rest were on, or had recently been on, proven drugs: alendronate, denosumab (Prolia), or teriparatide. Those drugs have been tested in large trials with thousands of women and a comparison group. They cut new spine fractures by about half to two-thirds. With Prolia, for example, about 7 in 100 women on placebo had a new spine fracture over three years, compared with about 2 in 100 on the drug (Prolia prescribing information). That's the kind of proof the stem-cell approach doesn't have yet.

There's also a statistical trap. The women joined the study right after a run of fractures, and fracture rates often settle down after a bad stretch, even without a new treatment. So a big "before and after" difference can look more impressive than it really is.

The researchers are clear that the study was small. Their own university's announcement calls it a trial with only ten patients and no control group, not enough to prove the treatment works.

The One Fracture Has a Lesson

The only fragility fracture in the two years after treatment happened in a woman who had skipped her next scheduled Prolia shot six months before her infusion. The fracture came three months after the infusion, about nine months after that missed dose.

That timing fits a well-known problem. When Prolia is stopped without switching to another medicine, bone loss can rebound quickly, and spine fractures can follow. The FDA label notes new spine fractures as early as seven months after the last dose. I explain this in more detail in my Prolia post.

So if you're on Prolia, please don't stop it on your own, whether you're hoping for a new treatment or for any other reason. If you want to come off it, talk with your doctor about a plan for what comes next.

How Long Before This Could Be Available?

Most likely several years. The good news is that the research is moving forward. The Murcia team says a larger trial is expected to start in 2027, with bigger trials involving several hundred people to follow. To be approved, the treatment would almost certainly need to show fewer fractures in a large trial with a comparison group. That takes careful follow-up, plus regulatory review.

Many stem-cell treatments that looked promising in early studies haven't held up in larger trials. Even members of this research team have written that only a few large trials of these cells have shown the hoped-for benefit. I hope this one does better, and I'll be watching for the next results. For now, it's wise to think of it as several years away.

A Warning About Stem-Cell Clinics

Some private clinics are already pointing to this study in their advertising. The cells used in the trial are experimental, made under a research protocol, and clinics don't offer them.

No stem-cell product is FDA-approved for osteoporosis. The FDA's warning is blunt: if you're being charged for these products or offered them outside of a clinical trial, you're likely being deceived. The FDA has received reports of serious harms from unapproved stem-cell products, including blindness, tumors, and infections.

If a clinic offers you stem cells, ask for the FDA study number (called an IND number) and a consent form that names the independent review board overseeing the study. A real clinical trial of these products should have both. Check what you're told with your own doctor before agreeing to anything.

What I'd Do Now

If you have osteoporosis, the best thing you can do today is start, or stay on, a treatment that's already been proven to prevent fractures. Pair it with strength training, enough protein and calcium, and fall-proofing at home. If this research pans out, it may one day add to what we have. Your bones need protecting in the meantime.
