The first Thanksgiving after my diagnosis, I caught myself staring at the table and running numbers. How much calcium was in the green bean casserole? Was the gravy too salty? Should I skip the wine?

I'd like to save you that particular brand of holiday stress. One meal, even a big one, isn't going to make or break your bones. What matters is what you eat most days of the year. So enjoy the turkey. And if you want to tilt the meal a little in your bones' favor, there are a few easy ways to do it, plus a couple of things worth knowing about salt, alcohol, and coffee.

The biggest holiday risk to your bones isn't on the plate, though. I'll get to that at the end.

A quick note for readers outside the U.S. Thanksgiving is an American holiday, but nothing here is specific to it. The same advice works for Christmas dinner, Diwali, Eid, Lunar New Year, or any big family meal where the food is rich and the wine is flowing.

What's Already Good on the Table

A traditional Thanksgiving dinner is friendlier to bones than you might think.

Turkey is protein, and bones need protein. Protein is part of the structure of bone itself, and it helps you keep the muscle that protects you from falls. Many of us over 50 don't eat as much as we should. A good serving of turkey goes a long way. I wrote more about how much protein to aim for in my post on protein and calcium for bone health.

The vegetables help, too. Sweet potatoes, squash, green beans, and Brussels sprouts bring potassium along with other nutrients. That matters because, as the National Academies' review of calcium points out, adding potassium to a salty diet may help your body hold on to calcium instead of losing it in your urine (Institute of Medicine, 2011).

Anything made with milk, cheese, or yogurt counts. Dairy is still the easiest way for most people to get calcium. An 8-ounce cup of plain low-fat yogurt has about 415 milligrams, and a cup of milk has around 300 (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements). For comparison, women over 50 need 1,200 milligrams a day from all sources.

A Vegetarian or Vegan Holiday Plate

No turkey? You can still cover protein and calcium. A serving of Tofurky roast (one-fifth of the roast) has about 33 grams of protein, close to a generous serving of turkey (Tofurky roast label). A lentil loaf or tempeh works well, too. For calcium, look for firm tofu made with calcium sulfate, about 253 milligrams per half cup, and use calcium-fortified soy milk (about 299 milligrams per cup) in the mashed potatoes or pie (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements).

One quick note on spinach. It does contain calcium, but your body absorbs very little of it, because spinach also contains a compound that binds calcium up. The NIH puts it at about 5 percent absorbed from spinach, compared with about 27 percent from milk. Spinach is still a good vegetable. Just don't count it as a calcium source.

Easy Ways to Add a Little Calcium

You don't need a special menu. A few small changes to dishes you're already making will do it.

  • Mashed potatoes: Use milk instead of water, and stir in a spoonful of plain Greek yogurt or a little grated Parmesan.
  • Pumpkin pie: The classic recipe already uses evaporated milk, which carries more calcium than regular milk because some of the water has been cooked off. Your grandmother's pie was doing you a favor.
  • Pie topping: Try a dollop of plain or vanilla Greek yogurt instead of, or alongside, whipped cream.
  • Appetizers: A cheese plate is a calcium plate. Hard cheeses like Parmesan, Swiss, and cheddar are especially good.
  • Leftovers: A turkey sandwich with a slice of cheese, or turkey soup finished with a splash of milk, keeps it going the next day.

If you don't eat dairy, calcium-fortified plant milks and calcium-set tofu can stand in. My bone health grocery guide has more ideas.

A Word About Salt

Holiday food is salty. Gravy, stuffing, ham, store-bought rolls, and anything from a box or can tend to carry a lot of sodium.

That matters for bones. When you eat more sodium, your kidneys get rid of the extra, and they let more calcium go with it. The National Academies' review is direct about it: "High intakes of sodium increase urinary calcium excretion" (Institute of Medicine, 2011). Most of what we know comes from measuring calcium in urine, not from counting fractures, so I wouldn't lose sleep over one salty meal. Over a whole season of holiday parties, though, it adds up.

A few easy habits help. Taste before you salt. Lean on herbs, garlic, lemon, and pepper. Make gravy from drippings with low-sodium broth. And fill part of your plate with those potassium-rich vegetables.

Alcohol: Where the Line Is

A glass of wine with dinner is fine for most people. The research shows the risk starts climbing above about two drinks a day.

The best evidence comes from a large analysis that pooled data from three international studies, nearly 17,000 men and women. People who had up to two units of alcohol a day didn't have a meaningfully higher fracture risk. Above two units a day, the risk of breaking a hip was about 70 percent higher (Kanis and colleagues, 2005). The FRAX fracture risk calculator, which many doctors use, asks whether you have three or more units a day for this reason. You can read more about it in my FRAX post.

The catch is that a "unit" is smaller than the drinks most of us pour. FRAX defines it as about one medium glass of wine (120 milliliters, about 4 ounces), one regular beer, or one single shot of liquor (FRAX). A generous holiday pour of wine can easily be one and a half or two units.

Alcohol also affects your balance, and that's the more immediate problem on a holiday evening. If you take medications that make you drowsy or dizzy, alcohol can make that worse. My post on medications that raise fall risk covers the common ones.

Coffee With Your Pie

Good news here. Coffee after dinner is fine.

The best long-term study followed more than 61,000 Swedish women for about 20 years. Women who drank more coffee did not break more bones, including hips. Very heavy coffee drinkers, four or more cups a day, had slightly lower bone density (2 to 4 percent), but it didn't translate into more fractures (Hallström and colleagues, 2013).

Caffeine does make you lose a little extra calcium. The National Academies notes that two to three or more cups a day has been linked to bone loss, but only in people who weren't getting enough calcium or milk (Institute of Medicine, 2011). So have your coffee, and add milk if you like it that way.

Cola is a little different. In the Framingham study, women who drank cola every day had lower bone density at the hip than women who rarely drank it. Diet cola showed the same pattern. Other carbonated drinks, like ginger ale or seltzer, did not (Tucker and colleagues, 2006). That study shows a link, not proof that cola causes bone loss, but if you're choosing a fizzy drink for the table, sparkling water or ginger ale is the safer bet.

The Real Holiday Risk: Falls

If I could change one thing about how people with osteoporosis spend Thanksgiving, it wouldn't be the menu. It would be paying attention to falls.

For most of us, a broken bone starts with a fall, and the holidays stack the odds. You're in someone else's house, with unfamiliar stairs and dim hallways. There are extra rugs, extension cords, coats piled by the door, grandkids' toys on the floor, and a dog underfoot. You may be carrying a heavy dish. You may have had a glass of wine. And it's late in the evening when you head out to the car, possibly in the dark, possibly on ice.

A few things make a real difference:

  • Let someone else carry the turkey. A hot, heavy roasting pan held out in front of you is exactly the kind of load your spine doesn't need. If you do lift, use the hip hinge from my squats and lifting post and keep the weight close to your body.
  • Use the handrail. Every time, even for three steps.
  • Ask for a light. It's fine to ask your host to leave a hall or bathroom light on overnight. Bring a small night-light if you're staying over.
  • Wear real shoes. Supportive shoes with good grip, not socks or slippers on hard floors.
  • Take your time getting up. After a long meal, stand up slowly and get your balance before you walk.
  • Take a walk after dinner. If the weather and the ground cooperate, a walk is good for your bones, your balance, and your digestion. Skip it if it's icy.

My guide to fall-proofing your home has more ideas, and many of them work just as well when you're the guest.

Enjoy the Day

Enjoy your Thanksgiving. Eat the turkey, have the pie, and don't feel guilty about either. If you want to help your bones a little, add some dairy where it fits, go easy on the salt shaker, keep the wine to a glass or two, and pour your coffee with milk.

Then hold the handrail on the way out.

If you have questions about how your own medications, alcohol, or diet fit together, that's a good conversation to have with your own doctor.
