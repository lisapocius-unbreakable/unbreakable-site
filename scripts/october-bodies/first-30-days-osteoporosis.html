Maybe it was a phone call from your doctor's office. Maybe it was a message in your patient portal, with a T-score and a word you'd been hoping not to see. However it arrived, you now have a diagnosis of osteoporosis, and a question that people type into search engines every day: now what?

I've been there, though I came at it from a harder direction. My diagnosis came after spine fractures, not a routine scan. What I wanted most in those early weeks was a sense of order. What should I do first? What could wait?

Chapter 12 of my book lays out a month of concrete steps, and this post walks through the heart of that plan. A month is long enough to do this properly, and short enough that nothing important gets put off.

Why a Plan Matters

Two things tend to happen after a diagnosis. Some people try to fix everything in a weekend. They buy every supplement on the shelf, sign up for a boot camp, and burn out by week two. Others freeze. The appointment gets pushed back, the DEXA report sits in a drawer, and months go by.

The second one worries me more. In a study of nearly 97,000 Americans hospitalized for a hip fracture, fewer than one in three started an osteoporosis medication within the following year (Solomon and colleagues, 2014). That's people who had already broken a hip. Osteoporosis doesn't hurt until something breaks, so it's easy to let it slide.

You don't need to do everything at once. You do need to start.

This plan assumes you were diagnosed through a screening scan, without a recent fracture. If you're recovering from a fracture, skip down to the section near the end. Your first month looks different.

Week 1: Get Your Information Together

Get your numbers. Ask for a copy of your full DEXA report, including the pages behind the summary. You want your T-scores for every site that was measured, usually the spine and hip, along with your Z-score. If your report includes a FRAX score, which estimates your chance of a fracture over the next ten years, write it down. Ask whether a TBS score, a measure of bone quality, was included. My posts on FRAX and what TBS and VFA mean explain these in plain terms.

Make the appointment. Book a visit with your primary care doctor or an endocrinologist to talk about treatment, ideally within two to four weeks. If you've had bloodwork in the past year or two, such as a vitamin D level, calcium, or thyroid tests, gather those results.

See your dentist. If it's been more than a year since your last checkup, schedule one. Some bone medications make it important to have dental work done before you start them, and it's much easier to handle this now than to delay treatment later.

Start a bone health notebook. This can be paper or a note on your phone. In it, record:

  • Your current height, measured standing against a wall. Height loss over time can be a sign of spine fractures you didn't know you had.
  • Every medication and supplement you take, including over-the-counter ones.
  • Any family history of osteoporosis, fractures, or a stooped posture in a parent or grandparent.
  • Any symptoms, like back pain.
  • Questions as they come to you.

That notebook is going to make your appointment much more productive.

Week 2: Small, Safe Changes at Home

Look at your home with fresh eyes. Most fractures start with a fall, so this is one of the highest-value things you can do. Walk through each room looking for things you could trip on. Start with the quick fixes: remove throw rugs, brighten dim hallways and stairs, and clear clutter from walkways. Check that you have sturdy handrails where you need them. My room-by-room fall-proofing guide goes through it in detail.

Start moving gently. If you aren't already walking, begin with ten to fifteen minutes a day. Practice standing on one foot while holding on to the kitchen counter, working up to 30 seconds on each side. Start noticing your posture through the day.

Until you've talked with your doctor or a physical therapist about exercise, keep things gentle. Hold off on high-impact activities, and avoid bending forward from your waist or twisting hard, especially while you're carrying something. Once you've had that conversation, most people can do much more than they expect. My post on squats, lifting, and bending explains how.

Take an honest look at your plate. For three or four days, jot down what you eat and drink. Then estimate your daily calcium and protein. My free calcium and protein cheat sheet makes this easier. Note how much caffeine, alcohol, and soda you're having too. Then pick just one change for this week. Swap an afternoon soda for water, or add a cup of yogurt to breakfast. One change you keep beats five you abandon.

Week 3: Your Appointment and Your Plan

This is the week the medical plan takes shape. Bring your notebook and your questions. My post on your first osteoporosis visit covers what a good workup looks like in detail. The short version follows.

First, your doctor should look for anything that might be causing or speeding up your bone loss. Expect at least some basic blood tests, including a vitamin D level. Your doctor should also review your medication list. A surprising number of common medicines can weaken bone, and I've written a separate post on medications that can quietly weaken your bones.

Then comes the treatment conversation. Is medication recommended for you? If so, which one, and why that one? How long would you take it, and what are the realistic side effects? If medication isn't recommended yet, what's the plan, and when will your bone density be rechecked?

Let your results guide supplements. It's tempting to start a handful of supplements on day one. I'd wait for your bloodwork. Your vitamin D dose should depend on your actual level. And calcium is best taken from food first, with a supplement only to fill the gap your food diary shows. If you do take calcium pills, your body absorbs them best in doses of 500 milligrams or less at a time (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements), so split larger amounts across the day and take them with food.

If you're prescribed a medication, read the patient information and talk with your pharmacist. Some osteoporosis pills have very specific rules about timing, water, and staying upright afterward. My practical tips for taking bisphosphonates cover the most common ones. Set up a reminder system that works for you, whether that's a phone alarm or a pill organizer.

Week 4: Build Your Team and Your Routine

Know who's in charge. Decide who will manage your osteoporosis care. It might be your primary care doctor, an endocrinologist, or a rheumatologist. Ask for referrals if you need them.

See a physical therapist. If you can, book a visit with a physical therapist who works with osteoporosis. They can show you which exercises are safe and useful for you, check your form, and help you build a realistic weekly plan. A good starting goal is daily walking plus two or three days of strength training each week.

Set three-month goals. Keep them concrete. How many days a week will you exercise? What diet change will you make next? Which home safety fixes will be done?

Mark your calendar. Put your next appointment, any follow-up bloodwork, medication refills, and your next DEXA scan on the calendar now. Repeat scans are usually one to two years apart, but your doctor will tell you what makes sense for you.

Then take a moment to notice how far you've come in a month. You went from a frightening phone call to a real plan. That counts.

If You Got Here Through a Fracture

If your diagnosis came after a broken bone, healing comes first. In the first week or two, follow your doctor's activity limits closely, manage your pain, rest, and eat enough. Protein matters more than usual right now, and my post on protein after a fracture has easy ideas for when you don't feel like cooking.

Hold off on starting an exercise program until you're cleared. Don't try to fall-proof your home on your own while you're still unsteady. Ask someone to help.

Ask your doctor to check for other causes of bone loss, just as you would after a screening diagnosis. If you're younger than 65 or have any history of cancer, ask whether something more serious could be behind the fracture.

As the weeks go on and you start to feel better, you can gradually work through the steps above. My post on exercise after a fracture explains how to move safely once you're cleared.

About the Feelings

Nobody prepares you for this part. A diagnosis like this can make you feel suddenly older and more fragile than you were the day before. Some days you'll feel in control. Other days it will all feel like too much. Both are normal.

If you find yourself feeling down for more than a few days, tell a friend or your doctor. A counselor can help too. I wrote about my own experience with this in when osteoporosis changes how you see yourself.

Managing osteoporosis is a long project. The first 30 days won't solve everything, and they don't have to. They lay the foundation for everything that comes after. Be patient with yourself, and keep going one step at a time.
