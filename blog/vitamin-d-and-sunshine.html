Sunshine and Vitamin D: What Actually Works, and What Does Not

July 19, 2026 · By Lisa Pocius, MD

Few questions come up as often in bone health as the one about sunshine. People want to know whether they can skip the supplement if they just get outside more, and whether the vitamin D they make in the sun is somehow better than what comes in a capsule. These are good questions, and the honest answers are more interesting than a simple yes or no. So let me walk through the ones I hear most, one at a time.

A quick word on why this matters for your bones. Vitamin D is what lets your body absorb the calcium you eat. Without enough of it, even a calcium-rich diet cannot do its job, and your bones pay the price. That is why getting your vitamin D right sits near the center of any real plan for osteoporosis.

Is Sun-Made Vitamin D Different From a Supplement?

The vitamin D your skin makes in sunlight and the vitamin D3 in a good supplement end up as the very same molecule in your body. When ultraviolet B light, the part of sunlight known as UVB, strikes your skin, it turns a form of cholesterol sitting there into vitamin D3, the exact compound sold as cholecalciferol on supplement labels, as described in a review in the American Journal of Lifestyle Medicine. Once it is in your bloodstream, your body cannot tell where it came from.

There is one meaningful difference, and it works in the sun's favor. When you make vitamin D in your skin, your body has a built-in stop switch. Sunlight itself breaks down any excess before it can build up, so you cannot overdose on vitamin D from sun exposure alone. A supplement has no such brake, which is why it is possible, though uncommon, to take too much in pill form. Sunlight also does a few other useful things for you that a vitamin D pill simply cannot, and I will come back to those at the end.

Can I Get All My Vitamin D From the Sun in Summer?

In the right season and the right place, yes, quite possibly. Under a summer sun, with your face, arms, and legs uncovered, many light-skinned people make plenty of vitamin D in as little as five to thirty minutes a few times a week, according to that same lifestyle medicine review. On a bright day, skin can produce several thousand units in one short session, far more than any daily supplement provides.

The catch is that summer does not last, and the sun cooperates only part of the year in much of the country. What your skin can make in a few minutes in July can take far longer in the cooler months, and through the depths of winter at northern latitudes it may not happen at all. So getting your vitamin D from summer sun is realistic for a stretch of the year, and a supplement quietly fills the gap the rest of the time. In a genuinely sunny climate close to the equator, year-round sun is more achievable, though the same limits around midday timing and skin tone still apply.

Do Windows Block Vitamin D?

Yes, and this surprises a lot of people. Ordinary window glass lets in the warm, bright light you can see and feel, but it filters out almost all of the UVB rays that make vitamin D. Sitting by a sunny window, or in a car with the windows up, will not help your vitamin D at all, no matter how warm and pleasant it feels. To make vitamin D, sunlight has to reach your bare skin directly, out in the open air.

Does Sunscreen Block Vitamin D?

In a laboratory, sunscreen applied thickly and perfectly can indeed block much of the skin's vitamin D production. In real life, the picture is reassuring. When researchers looked at how people actually use sunscreen day to day, they found little evidence that it drops vitamin D to low levels, as summarized in a review indexed by the National Library of Medicine. Most of us apply sunscreen more thinly and less evenly than the studies do, so some UVB still slips through, and people who use sunscreen tend to spend more time outdoors overall.

The takeaway I give patients is simple: do not skip sun protection out of worry about vitamin D. Skin cancer is a real risk, and a supplement is a far safer way to top up your levels than a sunburn. If you like the idea of a little unprotected sun for vitamin D, keep it brief, well short of any pinkness, then cover up or apply sunscreen for the rest of your time outside.

What About Clouds, Season, Latitude, and Altitude?

All of these change how much UVB actually reaches your skin, which is really the heart of the whole subject. It helps to picture UVB as a resource that is plentiful some days and scarce on others.

  • Clouds. Thin or scattered clouds let a good deal of UVB through, so a slightly overcast summer day still counts. Heavy, dark overcast is another story and can block most of it.
  • Time of year. Summer sun rides high and delivers strong UVB; winter sun sits low and weak. Above roughly the latitude of the middle of the United States, the winter sun often produces little to no vitamin D for months at a time.
  • Time of day. The middle of the day, when your shadow is shorter than you are, is when UVB is strongest. Early morning and late afternoon sun feels lovely but makes very little vitamin D.
  • Nearness to the equator. The closer you live to the equator, the higher the sun climbs and the more UVB reaches you year-round. The same few minutes that suffice in Miami can be nowhere near enough in a northern city in winter.
  • Altitude. Higher up, the thinner air filters out less UV, so sunlight is more intense on a mountain than at sea level. That means both faster vitamin D and a faster burn, so the same caution applies.

What Does Skin Color Have to Do With It?

Quite a lot, and it is worth understanding without any judgment attached. The pigment that gives skin its color, melanin, is a natural sunscreen. It protects against sun damage, which is a real benefit, but it also means darker skin needs considerably more time in the sun to make the same amount of vitamin D as lighter skin. Where a fair-skinned person might make plenty in ten or fifteen minutes, someone with very dark skin may need several times as long for the same result. This is simply how the body works, and it is a big reason that vitamin D deficiency is more common in people with darker skin, especially those living far from the equator. For many, a supplement is the most practical answer.

Does Age Change Things?

It does, and this matters a great deal for the very people most concerned about their bones. As we get older, our skin becomes less efficient at making vitamin D from sunlight. By our later years, the skin may produce less than half as much vitamin D from the same dose of sun as it did in youth, a change documented in the research on vitamin D and aging. Add in the reality that many older adults spend less time outdoors, and you can see why deficiency is so common in the years when bones need the most support. This is one of the clearest reasons that a vitamin D supplement is so often recommended as we age.

Does General Health Matter?

Yes. A few common situations make it harder to reach healthy vitamin D levels from any source. Carrying extra body weight tends to lower blood levels, because vitamin D is fat-soluble and gets tucked away in fat tissue rather than staying available in the bloodstream, which is why some people need a higher dose to reach the same level. The liver and kidneys both play a role in turning vitamin D into its active form, so conditions affecting those organs can interfere. And certain gut conditions reduce how well vitamin D is absorbed from food and supplements. If any of these apply to you, it is worth having your level checked and talking with your doctor about the right dose for your situation.

If I Dress Modestly, Is There Benefit in Private Sun on My Arms or Legs?

There genuinely is. Vitamin D is made in whatever skin the sunlight actually touches, and the more skin you expose, the more you make in the same amount of time. People who keep most of their skin covered whenever they are outdoors are at real risk of low vitamin D, a pattern seen clearly in studies of those who wear concealing clothing, such as one indexed by the National Library of Medicine. So yes, if stepping into a private space, a walled garden, a screened porch, or a quiet backyard, lets you comfortably bare your forearms and lower legs to the midday sun for a short while, that is a perfectly sound way to help your levels. Even a modest patch of skin makes a real contribution, and choosing the stronger midday sun means you need less time. And if that is not practical or comfortable for you, a supplement does the same job dependably.

What Else Does Daily Sunshine Do for Me?

This is my favorite part, because it is where sunlight quietly outshines any pill. Vitamin D is only one of the good things that happen when sun meets skin.

When sunlight touches your skin, it releases a small molecule called nitric oxide into your circulation, and nitric oxide relaxes and widens your blood vessels. In careful studies, this gently lowered blood pressure, entirely separately from vitamin D, a line of research led by dermatologist Dr. Richard Weller and colleagues and reported by the University of Southampton. It is one reason blood pressure across a population tends to run a little lower in summer than in winter.

Morning light does something different and just as valuable. Bright daylight early in the day helps set your body's internal clock, and getting it has been linked with better sleep quality, a connection explored in work published in BMC Public Health. Sunlight also lifts mood for many people, part of why the short, dark days of winter leave so many of us feeling flat. And simply being outdoors in daylight usually means moving your body, which your bones and balance both appreciate. None of these gentle benefits come in a vitamin D capsule, which is exactly why I encourage getting outside even when your supplement has your levels well covered.

What I Hope You Take Away

Sunshine and supplements are partners, not rivals. The vitamin D you make in summer sun is the real thing, and getting outside is genuinely good for you in ways that go well beyond your bones. At the same time, sunshine is unreliable across the year, it weakens with age, and it asks more of darker skin, so a modest daily supplement is a sensible safety net for most people, especially through the winter months.

My own approach, and the one I suggest to patients, is to enjoy short, sensible time in the sun without letting yourself burn, take a steady vitamin D supplement to cover the gaps, and check your level with your doctor if there is any doubt. That combination gives your bones what they need while letting you soak up everything else the sun offers.
