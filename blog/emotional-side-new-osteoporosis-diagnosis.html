The First Weeks After an Osteoporosis Diagnosis: The Part Nobody Warns You About

Lisa Pocius, MD

December 1, 2026 · About a 6-minute read

When my MRI was finished, the technician ran over to help me off the table as if I were 100 years old. A little earlier, the same tech had casually asked me to "hop" up onto it. I didn't like that one bit. The next day, the results popped up on my phone. I had four compression fractures in my upper and middle back.

I was 55. I'd fallen a few months earlier when my goldendoodle, Finnegan, yanked me off my feet, and I'd told the man walking by that I was fine. After all, I wasn't an old woman. I wasn't frail. Then a radiology report told me otherwise, and the way that tech helped me down from the table told me how other people might see me now.

If you've just been diagnosed, you may be feeling some version of that. This post is about the first few weeks, when the news is fresh and the plan isn't in place yet. In March I wrote about how osteoporosis can change the way you see yourself over the longer haul. This is about the part that comes before that.

The Shock Is Normal, Even for a Doctor

I'd been a doctor for decades, and bone health simply wasn't on my radar for myself. I was active and busy. Osteoporosis was something I expected to think about in another few decades, if ever.

So if your first reaction was disbelief, you're in good company. Mine was "This can't be right; the test must be wrong." Knowing the medicine didn't protect me from a very human reaction to bad news about my own body.

If your report says osteopenia rather than osteoporosis, the confusion can be its own kind of hard. You're left wondering whether you should be worried or relieved. Both feelings make sense. Osteopenia and osteoporosis are points along the same spectrum, and many of the same everyday steps protect your bones either way.

The Waiting Can Be the Worst Part

One stretch I wasn't prepared for came right after the diagnosis, before anyone knew why it had happened. As a doctor, I knew that fractures like mine after a minor fall could come from a cancer, such as multiple myeloma, or another condition that weakens bone. I spent three months waiting for tests to come back. My fear of cancer kept me up at night, and it scared my family. Those weeks felt endless, full of anxious internet searches and planning for the worst.

My tests came back negative. But I don't want to pretend the waiting is easy. If you're in that stretch now, a few things may help:

  • Ask what each test is looking for, and when you'll hear back. A date on the calendar is easier to live with than an open-ended wait.
  • Limit the late-night searching. Search results can't tell you which possibility applies to you. Your doctor can.
  • Write your worries down and bring them to your appointment. It's fair to ask your doctor directly, "Could something else be causing this?"

Feeling Suddenly Older

Many of us know the feeling the MRI tech gave me. One day you're simply yourself. The next day, you're someone with a condition you associate with your grandmother.

That sense of loss was one of the things I least expected. Something about feeling weak in my bones, in my very core, was unsettling. I was afraid of falling. I started rethinking my activities. The loss was invisible, but it was real. I looked the same from the outside and felt different inside.

I also became hyperaware of my back. Reaching for something on a high shelf, or bending to pick up something I'd dropped, suddenly took conscious thought. If you're noticing the same thing, it doesn't mean you're overreacting. Your brain is adjusting to new information.

It's All Right to Grieve

Looking back, what I went through resembled grief. I was mourning the carefree relationship I'd had with my body and my sense that nothing like this could happen to me.

I had the denial I mentioned. Then came anger: "Why me? I've tried to take care of myself." Then bargaining: "If I do everything perfectly now, maybe I can completely reverse this." And on bad days, I'd think, "My body is failing me, and there's nothing I can do about it."

I want to say a word about that bargaining stage, because it can trip people up in the first weeks. Trying to do everything perfectly, all at once, is exhausting, and it sets you up to feel like a failure when life gets in the way. You don't have to fix everything this month. You just have to start. My post on your first 30 days breaks the practical steps into one manageable week at a time.

Acceptance came for me, too, eventually. At first it felt like resignation. Over time it became the place I could build from.

Small Things That Helped Me in the Early Weeks

None of these fixed my feelings overnight. Each one made the next day a little lighter.

Learning, a little at a time. For me, education worked like emotional medicine. The more I understood about how bone works and what treatments exist, the less frightening the diagnosis became. Vague fears turned into a concrete plan. I'd suggest taking it in small doses, though. Trying to learn everything in a weekend is overwhelming.

Telling someone. I was surprised by how many people in my own circle had dealt with a bone loss diagnosis or worried about one. Saying it out loud to one friend can make the whole thing feel less lonely.

Counting small wins. My first wall push-up without back pain felt monumental. So did my first long walk without needing a pain pill. Those weren't dramatic fitness achievements, but they were proof I was moving forward. Your small wins might look different. Notice them anyway.

Being honest about the fear of falling. Fear of falling became a constant background noise for me. It's worth naming, because that fear can quietly lead you to do less, and doing less can leave you weaker and less steady. Talking it through with your doctor or a physical therapist, and learning what's safe for you, can turn that fear into caution you can live with.

When the Feelings Need More Help

Feeling shaken in the weeks after a diagnosis is normal. But sometimes the sadness or worry doesn't lift. If you feel sad, empty, or down most of the day, nearly every day, for more than two weeks, or you've lost interest in things you usually enjoy, please tell your doctor. Those can be signs of depression. The same goes for worry that feels hard to control or keeps getting in the way of your days. Depression and anxiety are both treatable, and a counselor or a support group can help, too.

It Does Get Easier

When I wrote my book, about a year after my diagnosis, I could honestly say the initial shock and fear had eased with time and knowledge. The fear hasn't disappeared entirely. I still think twice before stepping onto an icy sidewalk. But that awareness has become caution I can live with, not something that runs my life.

If you're in the early days right now, be patient with yourself. You are not broken. You are not weak. You now know something about your bones that many people don't, and you can start acting on it now.
