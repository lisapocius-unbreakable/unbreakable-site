Holiday Heavy Lifting: Cooking, Cleaning, and Decorating With Osteoporosis

Lisa Pocius, MD

November 10, 2026 · About a 9-minute read

The holidays are hard on backs. In a normal week, most of us lift a bag of groceries and maybe a laundry basket. Then late November arrives, and suddenly there's a heavy roasting pan going in and out of the oven, a big platter hiding in the cabinet over the refrigerator, and bins of decorations waiting in the basement. It's the heaviest housework of the year, packed into a few weeks and often done in a hurry.

None of that means you should sit the season out. Heavy housework, gardening, and carrying shopping are everyday activities that can help build muscle strength, as long as you avoid bending all the way forward over and over. The goal is to do these jobs in a way your spine can handle, and to know which ones to hand off.

In October I wrote about the hip hinge and safe lifting in general. This post goes job by job through the holiday version.

A Quick Refresher Before You Start

Almost every tip below comes back to a few principles from that earlier post.

  • Think straight. Keep your upper back straight and your neck in line with your spine whenever you bend or lift. It's the key principle for every movement that involves bending and lifting.
  • Bend at your hips and knees, not your spine. That's the hip hinge.
  • Keep the load close. The farther a heavy thing is from your body, the harder your spine has to work.
  • Turn with your feet. Twisting your spine while holding something heavy is one of the movements to avoid.
  • Don't bend while you're holding something heavy. Lift first, then move.

In the Kitchen

The turkey and the roasting pan

This is the big one. A full roasting pan is heavy, hot, awkward, and held out in front of you at arm's length, often while you're bent toward a low oven. That's almost exactly the position spine-safety guidance tells us to avoid.

A few ways to make it easier:

  • Pull the oven rack out toward you before you lift, so the pan comes to you instead of you reaching in for it.
  • Hinge at the hips, or drop into a half kneel in front of the oven, rather than folding forward from the waist.
  • Have a clear landing spot on the counter right next to the oven, so you aren't walking or turning with a hot pan.
  • Let someone else lift it out. If anyone in the house is strong and willing, this is their job.

If you're cooking for a smaller group, a turkey breast or a couple of roasted chickens are much easier to handle than a whole bird.

Heavy pots and pans

A full stockpot of potatoes or pasta water can weigh more than you think. Instead of carrying it to the sink, bring the water to the pot. Fill it on the stove with a pitcher or a measuring cup. When it's time to drain, scoop the potatoes or pasta out with a slotted spoon or spider strainer, or use a pot with a strainer insert you can lift out a little at a time.

Store your heaviest cookware at counter height for the season, if you can. Keeping things at waist height spares you from lifting heavy things into high or low storage.

Bending to the oven and the dishwasher

You'll be in and out of the oven and the dishwasher a lot. Each trip is a small bend, and they add up.

For the oven, open the door, step to the side, and hinge or half kneel to check the oven or baste. For the dishwasher, the bottom rack is the low one. Stand close, bend your knees and hips, and load or unload a few items at a time onto the counter rather than lifting stacks of plates. When you put things away, step and turn with your feet instead of twisting from the dishwasher to the cabinet.

Getting the Good Dishes Down

Most of us keep the holiday platters, the gravy boat, and the big serving bowls somewhere hard to reach. Usually it's the cabinet over the refrigerator or the top shelf of the pantry.

Reaching up for a heavy object is risky in two ways. Lifting heavy things overhead puts downward pressure on the spine, and reaching far up to a high shelf can throw off your balance and lead to a fall.

What helps:

  • Get the holiday dishes down early, all at once, with help, and keep them at counter height until January.
  • If you use a step stool, choose a wide one with non-slip grips on the steps and the feet, and hold the object close to your body.
  • Never stand on a chair. The same goes for changing lightbulbs or cleaning windows. Ask for help instead.
  • Ask a taller relative to reach the heavy cast iron. Many of them are happy to have a job.

My own spine fractures came from a fall, not from lifting. So I take the step stool part seriously. A fall from even a low height can break a bone.

Cleaning Before the Guests Arrive

Vacuuming

Vacuuming is a classic example in osteoporosis guidance, because most of us do it with our feet planted, twisting back and forth from the waist. The fix is to step to turn, so your leading foot and your trunk face the same direction.

In practice, that means walking with the vacuum rather than reaching with it. Keep it close, use shorter strokes, and move your feet. A lighter stick vacuum is easier than a heavy upright, and a long hose attachment saves you from bending under furniture. For stairs, a small handheld is safer than balancing a full-size machine.

Making beds for guests

Stand close to the bed and bend your knees and hips. Work your way around the bed instead of reaching across it. Putting one knee on the mattress, as I mentioned in October, can help with tucking in sheets.

Moving furniture

If you need to rearrange the living room for extra seating, this is a job to give away. Get someone else to move the furniture.

Decorations

Holiday decorations live in the hardest places to reach: the attic, the top of the garage shelves, the back of the basement. The bins tend to be big, heavy, and stacked.

A little planning makes this much safer:

  • Split decorations into smaller bins. Two light bins beat one heavy one.
  • Label them, so you aren't lifting and opening several to find the one you want.
  • Keep at least one hand free for the stair railing. If a bin takes both arms, it's too big to carry up or down stairs.
  • Leave the attic ladder and the high garage shelves to someone else.
  • The same goes for climbing a ladder to hang outdoor lights. Gutters and rooflines are a fall waiting to happen.

When the season's over, consider storing the bins you use every year on a shelf at waist height. Next year's version of you will be grateful.

Carrying Groceries

The holiday grocery run can be the heaviest single load of the season. Cans of pumpkin, bags of potatoes, a turkey, a case of seltzer.

  • Ask the cashier to pack the bags lighter, and carry two smaller bags, one in each hand, rather than one heavy bag on one side.
  • At the car trunk, bend your knees, rest them against the bumper to brace yourself, and hinge at the hips to reach in, rather than leaning in with a rounded back. Then lift one bag at a time.
  • Use a folding cart or rolling crate to get things from the car to the kitchen.
  • Grocery pickup or delivery is a perfectly good choice for the biggest shopping trip.

What About the 10-Pound Rule?

You may have been told never to lift more than 10 pounds. The Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation's safe movement guidance still includes that limit. The UK consensus and the Too Fit To Fracture experts take a different view. They recommend teaching safe lifting technique rather than a fixed weight limit. As the Too Fit To Fracture panel put it, "It is not just the amount of weight lifted, but how the weight is lifted that influences future fracture risk."

As I explained in my squats and lifting post, I lean toward the technique approach for most people. A 10-pound grocery bag lifted with a rounded back can be riskier than a heavier one lifted close to your body with a good hinge. But the right number for you depends on your bones, your strength, and your fracture history. If you've had a spine fracture, or you've been told you have a fragile spine, your doctor or physical therapist may give you a specific limit for a while, and that limit is the one to follow.

Asking for Help Is Part of the Plan

Many of us are used to being the one who does everything for the holidays. It can feel strange to hand off the heavy jobs.

Try thinking of it this way. Most guests would rather carry the turkey than watch you struggle with it. Teenagers can haul bins. A son-in-law can reach the top shelf. Making a short list of heavy jobs ahead of time, and handing them out when people ask "What can I do?", gets everyone involved and keeps you out of trouble.

When Back Pain Is a Warning Sign

This is the part I most want you to remember. In people with osteoporosis, a spine fracture can happen with everyday movements like reaching, twisting, or even a cough or sneeze. So if you feel sudden back pain after lifting the roasting pan or pulling down a bin, don't automatically write it off as a pulled muscle, arthritis, or just getting older.

Pain from a spine fracture often:

  • Starts suddenly
  • Gets worse when you stand or walk
  • Eases when you lie down on your back

It's usually in the middle or lower back.

If that sounds like what you're feeling, call your doctor for further evaluation. Not every spine fracture hurts, either. Losing height or noticing your upper back is more curved can also be a sign, and it's worth mentioning at your next visit.

The American Association of Neurological Surgeons says to go to the emergency room if back pain comes with numbness or weakness (for example, in your legs), severe pain, a high fever, or any loss of control of your bladder or bowels. These are uncommon, but they can mean a nerve is under pressure or something else needs urgent care.

If you've had a spine fracture recently, the rules change for a while. The Royal Osteoporosis Society's guidance for clinicians advises temporarily avoiding heavy lifting for 12 weeks after a spine fracture, including carrying shopping, lifting pets or children, and gardening. When to start again depends on how bad the fracture and symptoms are. This may be the year someone else does the heavy holiday work. My post on exercise after a fracture covers what changes.

Enjoy the Season

The holidays are supposed to be about the people at the table, not about who carried the heaviest pan. Do the jobs you can do well, use your hips and knees, keep things close, and hand off the rest.

You'll still be the one who made it all happen.
