Osteoporosis Runs in Families: What to Tell Your Sister, Daughter, or Son

November 24, 2026 · By Lisa Pocius, MD

My maternal grandmother broke her hip. My mother lost three inches of height in her old age, and height loss like that can be a sign of small fractures in the spine. Put those together with my own spine fractures, and the family pattern is hard to miss.

Osteoporosis runs in families. If you have it, the people you share genes and family habits with may be at higher risk too. Thanksgiving is one of the few times a year many families are all in one room. It's a natural moment to share something that could help them, and you don't have to make it awkward.

Why Osteoporosis Runs in Families

Your bones are built from a blueprint, and a lot of that blueprint is inherited. Studies of twins and families show that genes account for somewhere between half and 85 percent of the differences in peak bone density from one person to the next (Ralston and Uitterlinden, 2010). "Peak bone density" means the most bone you'll ever have, usually reached in your 20s. Start lower, and you have less to lose before you reach the osteoporosis range.

There isn't one osteoporosis gene. A huge study of more than 400,000 people found over 500 places in our DNA that each nudge bone density a little, and all of them together explained only about a fifth of the differences between people (Morris and colleagues, 2019). That's why there's no simple genetic test for everyday osteoporosis. For now, your family history is the practical tool.

Families also share more than genes. They share recipes, activity levels, attitudes toward exercise, and sometimes habits like smoking. Those count too.

What the Research Shows

A parent's hip fracture is one of the best-studied risk factors in all of osteoporosis.

The largest analysis to date pooled data from 42 studies in 29 countries, more than 350,000 men and women. People whose mother or father had broken a hip were about 37 percent more likely to break a hip themselves. The link held up even after accounting for bone density, which means family history adds information a DEXA scan doesn't capture. It also held for brothers and sisters. A sibling's fracture carried about the same weight as a parent's (McCloskey and colleagues, 2025).

That's why the FRAX fracture risk calculator asks whether either of your parents broke a hip (FRAX). I explain how that tool works in my FRAX post. Notice that it only asks about parents. My grandmother's hip fracture doesn't count in the calculator. It still told me something about my family.

The effect may start early. In a small but often-cited study, daughters of women with osteoporosis spine fractures already had lower bone density in their spines than other women their age, while they were still years away from menopause (Seeman and colleagues, 1989). That suggests some of us start adulthood with less bone in the bank.

A family history raises your risk. It doesn't decide your future. It's a reason to pay attention, not a forecast.

Clues Your Family Might Not Call "Osteoporosis"

Many of our parents and grandparents were never tested or diagnosed. You may have to read between the lines.

The Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation points out that if a parent had noticeable height loss or a spine that curved forward, they may have had spine fractures (BHOF). Things worth asking the older relatives about:

  • Did anyone break a hip, wrist, or spine, especially from a simple fall?
  • Did a grandmother or great-aunt develop a "dowager's hump" or get shorter?
  • Did anyone break bones easily, even when younger?
  • Did anyone take steroids for a long time, for asthma, arthritis, or another condition?

Write the answers down. It's useful information to bring to your own appointments, and your relatives' doctors will want it too. My post on your first osteoporosis visit has more on what to bring.

Who in Your Family Should Get Checked

This is where it helps to be specific, and the current guidelines are fairly clear.

Sisters and other women 65 and older. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends bone density screening for all women 65 and older, whatever their family history (USPSTF, 2025). If your older sister has never had a DEXA scan, that's the first thing to mention.

Women past menopause who are younger than 65. The Task Force recommends screening for women in this group who are at higher risk, and it names a parent's hip fracture as one of the factors to consider, along with smoking, heavy drinking, and low body weight (USPSTF, 2025). Your osteoporosis isn't a parent's hip fracture, so it doesn't automatically qualify her. But it's worth telling her doctor about, so they can look at her whole risk picture.

Brothers, sons, and other men. Men get osteoporosis too, and they're tested far less often. The Task Force says there isn't enough evidence to recommend for or against routine screening in men. The Endocrine Society suggests testing for men 70 and older, and for men 50 to 69 who have additional risk factors (Endocrine Society guideline, 2012). I wrote a whole post on osteoporosis in men for exactly this reason.

Anyone who has already broken a bone as an adult from a minor fall. No matter their age or sex, that's worth a conversation with their doctor about whether osteoporosis played a role.

Where I Go Further Than the Guidelines

This is one place where my own view differs from the official recommendations, and I want to be upfront about that.

I think people with a strong family history should consider a bone density scan earlier than the guidelines suggest, as early as young adulthood, just to know their starting balance in the bone bank. The research doesn't specifically support this, so take it as my recommendation as a doctor who lives with this disease, not as a guideline. My reasoning is simple. A DEXA scan is quick and painless, and it uses less than a tenth of the radiation of a standard chest x-ray (RadiologyInfo). It isn't an expensive test. And a baseline makes every future scan more useful, because you can see how fast things are changing.

If your relative asks their doctor and the doctor says it isn't needed yet, I'd encourage them to gently persist. Something like: "Osteoporosis runs in my family. My sister has it, and our grandmother broke her hip. I'd like a baseline scan so we have something to compare to later." Insurance coverage for earlier scans varies, so it's worth checking first. A good doctor will take that request seriously.

The Younger Generation

Your children and grandchildren have something you and I don't: time to build bone.

According to the National Institutes of Health, most people reach their peak bone mass by their mid- to late 20s (NIAMS). The bone they build in their teens and 20s is the bone they'll draw on for the rest of their lives. That makes these years a real opportunity, especially for kids with a family history.

What helps most is pretty ordinary. The NIH points to the same basics:

  • Enough calcium and vitamin D, mostly from food. Milk, yogurt, and cheese are the best sources, and fortified foods help.
  • Weight-bearing and resistance activity. Running, jumping rope, dancing, gymnastics, and team sports like soccer and basketball all send the signal to build bone.
  • No crash dieting. The NIH warns that dieting, fasting, or over-exercising to lose weight during the teen years can lead to bone loss.

I'd add one more of my own: don't start smoking. Smoking is a well-known risk factor for osteoporosis, and the Task Force lists it.

You don't need to give a teenager a lecture on bone density. Saying "I have osteoporosis, and it can run in families, so the sports and the milk really do matter for you" may land better than you'd expect.

How to Bring It Up at the Table

Some families talk about health easily. Others would rather discuss anything else. A few things make it easier:

  • Keep it short. You don't have to share details of your diagnosis or treatment. Something like this is enough: "I found out I have osteoporosis. It runs in families, so it's worth mentioning to your doctor."
  • Pick your moment. A quiet word while you're doing dishes may go over better than an announcement over the turkey.
  • Talk to the people who matter most. Siblings, adult children, and nieces or nephews whose parent you share are the ones to start with.
  • Make it about them, not you. Most people respond better to "I want you to stay healthy" than to a list of statistics.
  • Point them to their own doctor. Your job is to share the family history. Whether they need a scan is a conversation for them and their doctor.

If your family gets defensive, let it go for now. You've planted the idea, and you can come back to it later.

A Gift That Doesn't Need Wrapping

I can't change the genes I passed down. Neither can you. But telling the people you love about your diagnosis gives them something useful: a reason to get checked a little sooner, or to take their bones a little more seriously while there's still time to build them.

It may be the most helpful thing you say all weekend.
