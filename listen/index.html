Chapter 6: The Hidden Thieves

Medications and conditions that can quietly cost you bone.

Here’s a guide to medications that may affect bone health. This list isn’t exhaustive, and the presence of a medication here does not mean you should stop taking it. What’s important is being aware of the potential bone effects, so you and your healthcare team can use appropriate monitoring (DEXA scans) and protective strategies (exercise and diet).

Medication CategoryExamplesWhat to Know
Glucocorticoids/corticosteroidsPrednisone (Deltasone), methylprednisolone (Medrol), dexamethasone (Decadron)Even short courses can affect bones; long-term use causes dramatic bone loss
Aromatase inhibitorsAnastrozole (Arimidex), letrozole (Femara), exemestane (Aroma sin)Used for breast cancer; eliminate estrogen’s bone protection
Androgen deprivation therapyLeuprolide (Lupron), goserelin (Zoladex), degarelix (Firmagon)Used for prostate cancer; eliminate testosterone’s bone protection
Hormonal contraceptives (injectable)Medroxyprogesterone (Depo-Provera)FDA black box warning for bone loss; greatest risk with long-term use
SSRIsFluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, citalopram, escitalopramRisk may increase with duration, especially after 5+ years
SNRIsVenlafaxine, duloxetineSimilar possible risks to SSRIs
Anti-seizure medicationsPhenytoin (Dilantin), carbamazepine (Tegretol), phenobarbital (Luminal), valproic acid (Depakote)Enzyme-inducing drugs particularly affect bone metabolism
Proton pump inhibitorsOmeprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazoleRisk increases with long-term use (years, not months)

Chapter 8: Nutrition and Bone Health

What to eat, in the amounts that actually matter.

In the United States, results are typically reported in ng/mL (nanograms per milliliter). Here’s what those numbers mean:

Vitamin D LevelStatusGuidelines and Clinical Notes
Below 12 ng/mL (30 nmol/L)Severe deficiencySupplementation shows the clearest bone health benefits in this range.
12-20 ng/mL (30-50 nmol/L)DeficientSupplementation is strongly recommended to reach safe levels.
20-30 ng/mL (50-75 nmol/L)InsufficientMay benefit from supplementation, especially for protecting bone health.
30-40 ng/mL (75-100 nmol/L)AdequateGenerally considered adequate for maintaining bone health.

Chapter 9: Medication Choices

The osteoporosis medications, side by side.

Understanding the side effects is reassuring for most people. Serious side effects are uncommon. The most common issues involve stomach upset with oral forms of the medication and flu-like symptoms after IV infusions. Two rare but serious concerns with long-term use are osteonecrosis (death of bone) inside the jaw, which is more common in people receiving high-dose IV bisphosphonates for cancer or those who undergo teeth extraction, and atypical femur fractures, which are unusual breaks in the thigh bone. Both are very rare in people taking standard doses for osteoporosis. Osteonecrosis of the jaw has been reported in 1 in 100,000 to 1 in 1,000 patients, with the higher rates seen in long-term users. This helps to explain why some doctors recommend a “drug holiday” after several years of treatment.

Medication and UsageMechanism and Side EffectsImportant Notes
Alendronate (Fosamax, Fosavance, Vantavo) Pill: Daily or weekly Cost: LowAction: Slows osteoclast activity; preserves existing bone. Side effects: Heartburn, stomach upset, esophageal irritation. Rare: jaw problems, unusual thigh fractures.Generic available. Must take on empty stomach and stay upright for 30–60 minutes. Reduces both vertebral and hip fractures by 40–50%.
Risedronate (Actonel, Atelvia) Pill: Daily, weekly,or monthly Cost: LowAction: Slows osteoclast activity. Side effects: Like alendronate, but may have slightly less gastrointestinal (GI) upset.Generic available; flexible dosing. Similar efficacy and administration rules to alendronate.
Ibandronate (Bonviva, Bondronat, Bondenza) Pill: Monthly IV: Every 3 months Cost: Low to mediumAction: Slows osteoclast activity. Side effects: Pill: Similar GI effects to alendronate. IV: Flu-like symptoms for 1–3 days post-infusion.Less frequent dosing. IV form avoids GI side effects. Proven benefit mainly for spine fractures.
Zoledronic acid (Reclast, Aclasta, Zometa) IV infusion: Once yearly Cost: MediumAction: Slows osteoclast activity. Side effects: Flu-like symptoms (fever, aches) 1–3 days after first dose. Rare: kidney problems.Convenient yearly dosing. No GI side effects. Requires adequate hydration and kidney monitoring. Very effective for all fracture types, with up to a 70% reduction in vertebral fractures.

The potential downsides of denosumab include the potential for low blood calcium levels (after all, bone breakdown is the body’s way of rapidly increasing blood calcium levels), especially in people with kidney problems, and the need for careful planning around any treatment discontinuation. Stopping denosumab suddenly can lead to rapid bone loss, so transitions to other medications require careful timing and monitoring.

Medication and UsageMechanism and Side EffectsImportant Notes
Denosumab (Prolia, Xgeva) Injection: Every 6 months Cost: Medium to highAction: Blocks signals that activate osteoclasts. Side effects: Low calcium, skin infections, back/muscle pain. Rare: jaw problems, unusual thigh fractures.No stomach issues. Excellent bone density improvements. Cannot stop abruptly (rapid bone loss occurs). Must transition to another drug if stopping.

I’ll be taking teriparatide for another year or two from the time of this writing. Anabolic medications used to have a firm two-year lifetime use restriction based on early studies in rats, but this limitation was removed by the FDA in November 2020 after real-world evidence showed no increased cancer risk in humans. However, most treatment courses still last 18–24 months, as this represents the time frame for optimal bone-building benefit. The important consideration is that anabolic medications work best when followed by antiresorptive therapy to maintain the bone gains achieved. Without this follow-up treatment, much of the benefit can be lost over time.

Medication and UsageMechanism and Side EffectsImportant Notes
Teriparatide (Forteo, Forsteo) Daily self-injection Cost: Very highAction: Stimulates osteoblasts; actively builds new bone. Side effects: Nausea, leg cramps, dizziness. Temporary high calcium levels.Most potent bone-builder. Increases spine density significantly. Treatment typically 18–24 months. Must follow with antiresorptive to maintain gains. Available as a generic with a modest reduction in cost.
Abaloparatide (Tymlos, Eladynos) Daily self-injection Cost: Very highAction: Stimulates osteoblasts; actively builds new bone. Side effects: Dizziness, nausea, headache, palpitations.Like teriparatide. Treatment limited to 2 years. Must follow with antiresorptive to maintain gains.
Romosozumab (Evenity) Injection: Monthly for 12 months Cost: Very highAction: Dual-action: increases bone formation and decreases resorption. Side effects: Joint pain, headache. Caution: May increase risk of cardiac events.Rapid density gains. Limited to 12 months. Reserved for those with very high risk. Not for recent heart attack/stroke patients. Must follow with antiresorptive.

The downsides include hot flashes, which can be problematic for women still experiencing menopausal symptoms, and an increased risk of blood clots (similar to estrogen therapy). Leg cramps are another common complaint; some women find these bothersome enough that they discontinue treatment.

Medication and UsageMechanism and Side EffectsImportant Notes
Raloxifene (Evista) Pill: Daily Cost: Low to mediumAction: Acts like estrogen in bones; blocks estrogen in breast tissues. Side effects: Hot flashes, leg cramps, increased blood clot risk.Reduces vertebral fractures ~35% (no proven hip benefit). Bonus: Reduces breast cancer risk ~35%. Not for women with hot flashes.

If you’re considering HRT, the conversation with your doctor should include your individual risk factors for breast cancer, heart disease, and blood clots, as well as the severity of your menopausal symptoms and your fracture risk. The decision is highly personal and needs you to weigh multiple factors unique to your situation.

Medication and UsageMechanism and Side EffectsImportant Notes
Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) Pill, patch, or cream: Daily Cost: LowAction: Replaces estrogen to maintain bone balance. Side effects: Breast tenderness, bloating. Risks: Clots, stroke, heart disease.Reduces all fractures by 20–40%. Only drug effective at all risk levels. Benefits stop when medication stops. Best for women with menopausal symptoms.

If you’re currently taking calcitonin for osteoporosis, it’s worth discussing with your doctor whether more effective alternatives might be appropriate for you.

Medication and UsageMechanism and Side EffectsImportant Notes
Calcitonin Nasal spray or injection Cost: LowAction: Temporarily inhibits osteoclasts. Side effects: Nasal irritation, nausea, flushing.Rarely used. Modest spine benefit only. Small potential cancer risk with long-term use. Less effective than other options.

Glossary, with pronunciations

Some of these words are hard to say out loud. The guides in parentheses show where the stress falls.

Abaloparatide (a-bal-oh-PAR-ah-tide)
An anabolic (bone-building) medication (brand name Tymlos) given as a daily injection.
Alendronate (ah-LEN-droe-nate)
A bisphosphonate medication (brand name Fosamax) taken orally to treat osteoporosis.
Bisphosphonates (bis-FOS-foe-nates)
A class of medications that slow bone loss by inhibiting osteoclast activity.
Calcitonin (kal-sih-TOE-nin)
A naturally occurring hormone that slows bone breakdown. Synthetic salmon calcitonin was once prescribed as a nasal spray for osteoporosis, but it has largely been replaced by more effective medications.
Cancellous bone (KAN-sell-us)
The spongy, honeycomb-like interior bone tissue; also called trabecular bone.
Denosumab (den-OH-sue-mab)
A biologic medication (brand name Prolia) given by injection every six months to slow bone loss.
Estrogen (ES-troh-jen)
A hormone that plays a key role in maintaining bone density. Estrogen therapy, sometimes combined with other agents, is used to prevent bone loss in postmenopausal women.
Hyperparathyroidism (high-per-para-THY-royd-ism)
A condition in which one or more parathyroid glands produce too much parathyroid hormone, leading to increased bone breakdown.
Ibandronate (eye-BAN-droe-nate)
A bisphosphonate medication (brand name Boniva) available in monthly oral or quarterly intravenous forms.
Kyphoplasty (KYE-foe-plas-tee)
A minimally invasive procedure to treat vertebral compression fractures by inflating a balloon in the fractured vertebra and filling it with bone cement.
Kyphosis (kye-FOE-sis)
An exaggerated forward curvature of the upper spine, sometimes called a “dowager’s hump.”
Osteoblasts (OS-tee-oh-blasts)
Bone-building cells that create new bone tissue.
Osteoclasts (OS-tee-oh-clasts)
Bone-resorbing cells that break down old or damaged bone tissue.
Osteopenia (os-tee-oh-PEE-nee-ah)
A condition of mildly reduced bone density, between normal bone density and osteoporosis.
Osteoporosis (os-tee-oh-pore-OH-sis)
A condition in which bones become weak and brittle due to loss of bone density and mass.
Physiatrist (fiz-EYE-ah-trist)
A physician specializing in physical medicine and rehabilitation, trained to diagnose and treat conditions affecting the muscles, bones, nerves, and brain without surgery.
Raloxifene (ral-OX-ih-feen)
A selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) used to prevent and treat osteoporosis in postmenopausal women.
Risedronate (rih-SEH-droe-nate)
A bisphosphonate medication (brand name Actonel) taken orally to treat osteoporosis.
Romosozumab (roe-moe-SOE-zoo-mab)
A dual-action medication (brand name Evenity) given as a monthly injection that both builds new bone and slows bone loss.
Teriparatide (ter-ih-PAR-ah-tide)
An anabolic (bone-building) medication (brand name Forteo) given as a daily injection.
Thoracic (thor-AS-ik)
Relating to the chest region of the spine (the 12 vertebrae between the neck and lower back).
Trabecular bone (trah-BEK-you-lar)
Seecancellous bone.
Vertebrae (VER-teh-bray)
The individual bones that make up the spinal column.
Vertebroplasty (ver-TEE-bro-plas-tee)
A minimally invasive procedure to treat vertebral compression fractures by injecting bone cement directly into the fractured vertebra to stabilize it. Unlike kyphoplasty, no balloon is used to restore height before the cement is injected.
Zoledronic acid (zoe-leh-DRON-ik)
A bisphosphonate medication (brand name Reclast) given as an annual intravenous infusion.

About the author

I spent ten years in family practice before I fell in my own front yard at 55 and fractured four vertebrae. The diagnosis of osteoporosis came afterward, and so did the discovery of how much the textbook leaves out. I wrote Osteoporosis: The Book I Wish I'd Had When I Was Diagnosed for the person sitting where I sat.
